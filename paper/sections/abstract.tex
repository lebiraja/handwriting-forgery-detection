Disputed handwritten scripts, such as an exam answer sheet suspected of being copied in another person's hand, require deciding whether a questioned page is consistent with a writer's earlier pages, yet most deep verifiers compare single words or signatures. We study page-level, multi-reference verification: a full page is embedded as an attention-pooled bag of ink patches by a ResNet-18 trained from scratch with content-matched hard negatives and distillation from a frozen DINOv2 teacher, and one or several reference pages are fused into a calibrated three-way verdict (consistent, inconclusive, likely forged). We train three seeds on IAM and CVL with writer-disjoint splits and test exactly once on held-out writers and on Firemaker, a separate corpus never used for training or tuning. The model reaches 2.35\% EER on IAM and 7.30\% on CVL, against 5.44\% and 15.02\% for frozen DINOv2 features, 11.1\% and 23.1\% for HOG, and 23.2\% and 30.6\% for an untrained network. On Firemaker, ordinary writing transfers across corpora (3.3\% EER), but upper-case copying (23.9\%) and deliberately disguised writing (32.0\%) break it. On one fixed set of writers, three reference pages lower the centroid-fusion EER from 2.25\% to 1.22\% on IAM and from 6.76\% to 5.19\% on CVL, and the maximum rule alone reaches 3.31\% on CVL, a post-hoc observation. A verdict calibrated on validation data leaves 8.5\% of CVL trials inconclusive and is wrong on 2.8\% of the rest. No ablated training ingredient shows a clear benefit in a short, single-seed, 40-epoch comparison. No public data show one person imitating another, so these are not forgery detection rates, and the font-rendered pages we tested say nothing about learned imitation.
